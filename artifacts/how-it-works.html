How We Got Here

Six days, four species, about twenty experiments, and two moments where the result turned out to be the opposite of what we'd announced. This is the whole thing explained with pictures — what we asked, what we found, and where we were wrong.

step 1

The question we could actually answer

The question we started with was can a dog and a cat understand each other. That isn't answerable — nobody can read meaning out of a sound. So we swapped it for one that is: given a recording, can a machine tell what situation the animal was in?

step 2

The mistake everybody makes

To test a model you split your recordings into a training half and a testing half. Almost everyone shuffles the clips at random. With animal recordings, that quietly breaks the test.

What that costs, measured

Doing it the honest way drops reported accuracy by 10 to 24 points, in every dataset and every feature set we tried. And when we asked the same numbers "which cat is this?", they answered correctly 79% of the time — against a 5% chance rate. The shortcut isn't hypothetical; it's the loudest thing in the data.

The result that surprised us most

A set of 88 hand-designed sound measurements from 2015 matches a 95-million-parameter neural network on pig emotion — once you test honestly. Under the shuffled test the neural net looks 6 points ahead. That lead lives entirely inside the broken metric.

step 4

The confidence numbers break the same way

There's a statistical method that promises "these predictions are 90% reliable." We ran it. On average it delivered exactly that. Per animal, it didn't.

step 5

Can we just delete the "who"?

If identity is the problem, erase it. Picture the 768 numbers as a space you can move around in. Some directions in that space carry "which animal"; delete those directions and keep the rest.

Correction: we announced the opposite of this

Earlier in the week we published — here, in two commit messages, and in both repositories — that "individual identity is removable but recording-site identity is not." That second half is false. Site identity is removable, at rank 5. Our first method was simply the wrong tool, and we mistook its failure for a property of the data.

step 6

But it comes back

After erasing the site information, a straight-line probe genuinely can't find it. A slightly cleverer one can — 92% of the time. Here's what it's seeing.

step 7

The pattern we didn't expect

Five separate times, the simple option beat the sophisticated one — but only once we tested honestly.

  1. 88 hand-made features from 2015 matched a 95M-parameter neural network on pig emotion.
  2. Slowing bat recordings down 4× beat a purpose-built multi-band method from a published paper.
  3. A 30-line linear projection beat a trained adversarial neural network at removing identity — 2–3× more removed, and it damaged the task less.
  4. Using no pitch feature at all beat every pitch-based idea we had, including our own flagship one.
  5. A closed-form formula beat the iterative method it replaced, reaching the floor in 5 steps where the other stalled forever at 32+.

Why this keeps happening

It isn't that complexity is bad. It's that the shuffled test rewards models that memorise the animal, and big models memorise better than small ones. Remove the shortcut and the advantage largely disappears. The sophistication was buying performance on the wrong task.

step 8

What we got wrong

Three claims we made confidently and then had to withdraw. Keeping the list is the point — if the project is about honest evaluation, it has to survive its own standard.

Withdrawn

  • "Site identity can't be removed." It can, at rank 5, in closed form. We'd used the wrong method and blamed the data.
  • "Body-size-normalised pitch will transfer across species." It doesn't — and it can't, because the pitch–emotion relationship flips sign between pigs and the others. Rescaling can't reverse a sign. That was a reasoning error, not a data problem.
  • "Half the cross-species effect is call duration." Removing duration barely dented it. Duration is a parallel cue, not the mechanism.

Also caught before it shipped

  • Two references pointed at the wrong paper, and one credited the wrong researcher — two people with similar surnames.
  • The two most damning quotes in the paper were uncited and from a different paper than we thought.
  • Five editorial notes — including literally VERIFY before submission — would have been typeset into the bibliography of a double-blind submission.
  • A protocol bug in our own earlier code produced a coverage number below chance, which is impossible and revealed the flaw.
step 9

What it all adds up to

The one-sentence version

Across cats, dogs, pigs and wild bats, the AI models the field relies on are answering "who is this and where was it recorded?" while being graded as though they answered "what is happening?" — and the fix costs one extra number in your results table.

The recommendation is small and cheap: split your data by animal or by site, never at random; and report how well your features identify the animal right next to how well they identify the behaviour. If the first number is high, the second is borrowing from it.

Everything here ran on a laptop with no GPU. Total compute cost: $0. Every number traces to a script in the repository, and the results that didn't work are written down next to the ones that did.

Companion to the full findings and the paper draft. Data: CatMeows (Ludovico et al. 2021), dog barks (Molnár et al. 2008), Soundwel (Briefer et al. 2022), Egyptian fruit bats (Prat et al. 2017).
